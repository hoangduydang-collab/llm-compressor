Method

What was measured, and what the numbers do and don't mean

Speed was measured with the benchmarks repo's performance layer: aiperf 0.8.0 driving synthetic traffic against each arm's OpenAI endpoint, engine held fixed (vLLM 0.24.0, identical serving flags, FP8 KV cache). The quant arms fit one 8×H100 node (TP8); BF16 requires two nodes (TP16/ray) — so equal throughput would already mean half the hardware cost. Two workloads:

  • Reasoning — 1,000 synthetic input tokens, 8k forced decode via ignore_eos (arms compared on identical decode lengths, isolating kernel speed from model verbosity); temp 0.6, thinking on; concurrency sweep 1 / 4 / 16 / 64.
  • Agentic — multi-turn with a growing, mostly-cacheable prefix, shape calibrated on M3 itself with tau2-bench (details below).

Comparability caveat

All arms run identical protocol on dedicated idle nodes — valid for arm-vs-arm decisions. The provider comparison below is matched on workload shape (single request, 1k prompt, decode rate after first token) but providers carry live multi-tenant load and unknown stacks — treat it as same-ballpark placement, not a ranking.

Agentic serving

Latency under a real multi-turn shape

Agentic traffic is multi-turn with a growing, mostly-cacheable prefix, so the workload shape matters. We calibrated the shape on M3 itself with tau2-bench (114 telecom tasks, 1,540 agent turns; our GPTQ endpoint as agent, DeepSeek-V4-Pro as user-sim): a 7,300-token cacheable system+tools prefix, 256 unique tokens per session, 14±3 turns, ~100 output tokens per turn. warm = sticky multi-turn sessions (real serving, KV reuse); cold = unique single-shot prompts (cache-defeated control). Same tiers as everywhere else: the in-house arms hold a 1-second p95 SLO to concurrency 16 (BF16, on double the hardware, to 32), while MXFP8 and cyankiwi fall out past concurrency 4. Two findings behind the table:

  • Prefix caching is worth ~3–4× on TTFT. Cold-mode TTFT is 0.6–2.7 s for the in-house arms (4.7 s for MXFP8 at conc 16), and no arm holds a 1s SLO past concurrency 1 uncached. Agentic serving without KV reuse is not viable at load, at any precision.
  • The cache dominates turn depth. Warm TTFT falls as conversations deepen (647 ms at turn 0 → 287 ms by turn 19 while context grows 8k → 20k tokens): deep turns are almost entirely cached prefix. vLLM 0.24 never reports cached_tokens, so caching was verified by a preflight double-prompt probe plus this warm-vs-cold delta.

Workload-shape provenance

The first perf pass ran with a shape borrowed from the M2.5 tau2 calibration; the M3 calibration (run in parallel) matched it on every structural knob — prefix 7,300 vs 7,200 tokens, 14±3 turns, 256-token unique context — and corrected only per-turn verbosity (230 → 100 tokens; M3 speaks more tersely per agentic turn). The agentic numbers above are from the re-run under the M3-calibrated shape; the correction moved warm TTFTs by ≤10% for the in-house arms and changed no ranking. Caveat: per-turn output is bimodal (p50 84 / p95 252), so a flat mean understates tail turns.

Why cyankiwi is faster per-user but broken

The community quant's +16% single-user decode edge comes from quantizing attention to 4-bit in 57 of 60 layers (~13 GB less weight traffic per token) on the most mature W4A16 kernel path. Our recipe deliberately keeps all attention, shared experts, and router gates at BF16. The quality report is the verdict on that trade: cyankiwi's checkpoint produces degenerate reasoning loops and the worst scores on every axis — a ~16% speed edge in exchange for a broken model. Where FP8 activations matter (prefill, high concurrency), the trade reverses and our arms win decisively.

Stage 1 — the free 7%

Production serves had been running with the shared-expert auxiliary stream disabled — a workaround for a flaky illegal-memory-access during CUDA-graph capture. The IMA was root-caused to the fork's graph capture dropping upstream's pre-cleanup torch.cuda.synchronize(); with the sync restored (LLMC_M3_CAPTURE_SYNC=sync), the overlap is on by default. Measured twice: interleaved A/B on one node (TPOT 8.45 → 7.83 ms, −7.4%) and same-profile suite windows (95.3 → 103.3 tok/s). Concurrency-64 barely moved — the shared expert is TP8-sliced and short, so overlap matters most at small batch.

Stage 2 — the kernel switch

Humming is a Hopper-native WGMMA/TMA W4A8-FP8 MoE kernel with direct GPTQ loading. Qualifying it required a patched side-install carrying four SHA-pinned fixes we made to the upstream kernel — a missing input-schema entry, a grouped-mode expert-bound bug, a missing PTX fence, and (the hard one) TMA stores that were never committed to an async group, so every store-wait was a no-op and the epilogue memory was overwritten mid-store. All four are still unfixed upstream; every serve attests the patched kernel fail-closed before benchmarking. Result: decode 23–35% faster than CUTLASS at every operating point, in exchange for worse loaded prefill (TTFT +12–29% at concurrency ≥16). The grouped scheduling variant never beats indexed on decode; CUTLASS keeps the loaded-TTFT crown.

Stage 3 — packed-K is a trade, and its adoption is an open call

Humming 0.1.11 auto-enables a packed-K dequant layout for exactly our config. Measured two independent ways (paired suite window + AA-style sweep) against a ±0.2% run-to-run noise floor: it costs ~4% at concurrency 1 (indexed; −1% grouped) and gains 1–3% TPOT at concurrency 16–64, up to +7–10% in the most weight-bound cells. Adopt for throughput-oriented serving, hold 0.1.10 for interactive latency, or adopt per-gemm — the decision is deliberately not made here.
