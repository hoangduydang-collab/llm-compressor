Which M3 quants are safe to ship — and how the AWQ regression was fixed

Paired quality evaluations of our in-house quants against the BF16 baseline. GPTQ and MXFP8 recover the baseline cleanly. AWQ recipe r5 carried a large, specific regression on reasoning tasks that turned out to be one mechanism — the model stops terminating its own thinking — not broad capability loss. Recipes r6 and r7 removed the cause and largely close the gap. Serving speed and cost are covered in the companion report, M3_OFFICIAL_PERF_RESULTS.html.

Bottom line

Read this if you have thirty seconds

Across seven tasks, GPTQ (W4A8) and MXFP8 are ship-grade — every task recovers ≥97.4% of BF16, token spend matches the baseline, and the handful of answer changes are symmetric noise. In-house AWQ recipe r5 was not shippable: it lost 30 points on GPQA and 6 on IFEval. But the damage was not "AWQ can't reason" — when it produced an answer, its accuracy matched BF16. The loss was that on hard prompts it fell into runaway thinking loops, exhausted the generation budget, and emitted nothing, which scores as zero. Doubling the budget to 64k did not fix it, and switching to MiniMax's recommended sampling recovered roughly half the stuck items — showing part of the gap was a decoding artifact rather than pure recipe damage. A community AWQ quant is worse than ours on every axis.

That regression is now fixed at the recipe level. The cause was an AWQ up→down fold; removing it (r6) and replacing it with a function-preserving gate-path fold (r7) restores near-baseline behaviour on both damaged tasks: GPQA recovery 71.7% → 98.7% (r6) and 104.4% (r7), token spend 2.19× → 1.13×/0.93×, and budget exhaustion 38.9% → 14.7%/10.6% against a 12.6% BF16 floor. Under plain greedy decoding r6 now reaches a lower non-termination rate than r5 achieved with sampling — the recipe fix did what the decoding workaround was doing. See the r6/r7 section; note these two runs cover the two damaged tasks only, so the seven-task breadth verdict below still describes r5.

Speed is the other half of the trade and is reported separately: see the performance report for throughput, latency under agentic load, and the comparison against commercial MiniMax-M3 providers.

Method

What was measured, and what the numbers do and don't mean

Every run uses the official benchmarks A/B pipeline: each quant is served on its own node and scored against the BF16 baseline. Four complementary runs:

  • full4 (07-20) — breadth. Four arms (BF16, GPTQ, MXFP8, AWQ r5) × seven tasks, 32k-token budget.
  • tok64k (07-21) — depth. Five arms (adds a community AWQ, cyankiwi W4A16) × the two most-damaged tasks (GPQA-Diamond, IFEval), 64k-token budget, with per-request token accounting.
  • tok64k-awqr6 (07-23) and tok64k-awqr7 (07-24) — the recipe fix. Same two tasks, same 64k budget and protocol, one AWQ arm each.
  • sampling probes (07-22, and 07-23 for r6) — greedy vs MiniMax-recommended sampling on the exhausted-item subset.

Decoding is greedy (temperature 0), the suite's reproducibility convention. Thinking is on (M3 is a reasoning model; the minimax_m3 parser strips the <think> block and scores only the final answer — which is why an exhausted, answer-less response scores zero). GPQA-Diamond is graduate-level science with long chain-of-thought; IFEval is verifiable instruction-following, but on a reasoning model it too runs through a full think phase before answering.

Comparability caveat

These scores are valid for quant-vs-quant and quant-vs-BF16 decisions — all arms run the identical protocol. They are not directly comparable to public leaderboards: we use greedy decoding (MiniMax's own recipe is temperature 1.0 / top_p 0.95) and a task subset. The greedy choice matters for the AWQ finding and is the subject of an open follow-up (below).

What the r6 / r7 runs do and don't establish

Three limits, stated so the r6/r7 numbers are not over-read. (1) They cover two tasks — GPQA-Diamond and IFEval, the two r5 damaged — not the seven-task breadth suite; no arm other than r5 has a full-breadth AWQ measurement. (2) The BF16 comparison uses the frozen 07-21 baseline (the _lm_eval result files in both run trees are dated 07-21), which is correct for pairing against a fixed reference but means the baseline was not re-measured alongside the candidate. (3) The generated reports carry "no noise floor provided; significance not assessed" — the deltas are point estimates from a single run per arm, so treat sub-2-point differences (notably r7's IFEval −3.9) as unresolved rather than established.

Root cause

One mechanism: reasoning that never terminates

The GPQA and IFEval regressions look like two problems but are one. On hard prompts the AWQ model enters its <think> phase and never converges — it burns the entire budget mid-thought and returns an empty answer. Three independent lines of evidence:

1 · Extra budget does not rescue it

Of the 74 GPQA items AWQ exhausted at a 32k budget, 63 were still exhausted at 64k — only 11 recovered, and 14 new ones broke. Accuracy moved 0.571 → 0.576 (flat). This is non-termination, not "needs more room." AWQ's mean GPQA completion length is 26,463 tokens vs BF16's 12,059.

2 · IFEval failure is 100% empties, not lost instruction-following

When AWQ actually answers an IFEval prompt, it passes at 0.892 — statistically identical to BF16's 0.901. The entire −8-point gap is the 9.1% of prompts that exhaust the budget and emit nothing. Counterfactually crediting those empties at BF16's answer-rate reconstructs 0.893 = BF16's score exactly. Instruction-following capability is intact.

3 · The exhausted transcripts are literal loops

Repetition analysis of the exhausted reasoning tails (zlib compression ratio; lower = more repetitive): BF16/GPTQ/MXFP8 sit at ~0.15 — coherent reasoning that simply overran. Our AWQ is 0.078 (semantic loops — re-deriving the same argument). The community AWQ is 0.004 — degenerate single-token spam (drop drop drop…, the the the…).

Recipe vindication

The external cyankiwi AWQ (W4A16) is worse than our r5 on every axis — more spend, more exhaustion (55.6% vs 38.9% on GPQA), lower score (0.455 vs 0.576), more one-sided flips (71/2 vs 50/5). The failure mode is intrinsic to AWQ on M3's long reasoning, and our recipe is the better AWQ of the two. The r6/r7 result below strengthens this: the loop was our fold's doing and we removed it, while cyankiwi's degenerate loops remain.

The fix — AWQ r6 and r7

Removing the up→down fold removes the loop

The root cause was located in our own recipe: AWQ was folding smoothing scales through M3's up→down path, which is not function-preserving across M3's clamped GLU ((clamp(up)+1)·glu). Two follow-up recipes were quantized and evaluated on the identical protocol — same two tasks, same 64k budget, same greedy decoding, same frozen BF16 baseline:

  • r6 — noupdown: the up→down fold is simply dropped.
  • r7 — gatealpha: the down-side smoothing is reintroduced through the gate path, where it is function-preserving. Requires the gate-alpha overlay in the serving vLLM (pipeline/slurm/patch_vllm_m3_serve.py).

The token-spend collapse is the proof

Scores alone would be ambiguous — a recipe can score well for unrelated reasons. The mechanism metrics are what identify this as the same defect being removed: completion-token spend falls from 2.19×/3.69× to 1.13×/1.17× (r6) and 0.93×/1.25× (r7), and budget exhaustion falls from 38.9%/9.2% to 14.7%/1.5% and 10.6%/1.9%. r5's excess spend was the loop; without the fold, the excess is gone. r7 even spends less than BF16 on GPQA and exhausts less often.

r6 under greedy beats r5 under sampling

r5 needed MiniMax's recommended sampling to pull non-termination from 76% down to 49%. r6 reaches 46% under plain greedy — and barely moves under sampling (44%), exactly like the healthy BF16 and GPTQ controls. That is the signature of a fixed model rather than a masked one: the decoding knob has stopped mattering because there is no longer a loop for it to break. r6 also has the highest "correct when it finishes" rate of any arm measured (95%, vs BF16 86% and GPTQ 90%), so the remaining exhaustion on this subset is the genuinely-hard tail every arm shares.

Which AWQ recipe to serve

r6 is the balanced choice — it recovers ~98.6% on both tasks with near-symmetric flips, and it is the arm with the corroborating sampling probe. r7 is the higher-variance choice: it beats BF16 on GPQA (+3.5 pts, flips 9✗/16✓) but gives back IFEval (−3.9 pts, flips 37✗/16✓), and it additionally requires the gate-alpha serving overlay. With no noise floor established, the r6-vs-r7 difference is not resolved; r7 is the arm carried into the performance work, where the two are speed-identical. Neither has a seven-task breadth measurement yet — that is the open item.

The r5 decoding diagnostic

Sampling broke the loops for our AWQ, not for the community quant — the clue that led to the fix

Read this as diagnosis, not as the remedy

This section is the r5-era probe. It established that r5's loops were breakable — which is what pointed at a recipe defect rather than intrinsic 4-bit damage — but sampling is no longer the answer to the regression. r6/r7 fixed it in the recipe, and r6 reaches a lower greedy non-termination rate (46%) than r5 achieved with sampling (49%). Kept because the mechanism evidence and the arm-by-arm controls are still the clearest account of what was wrong.

Reasoning models are known to loop under greedy decoding (DeepSeek-R1's card warns of it; MiniMax recommends temperature 1.0 / top_p 0.95), and our runs are greedy. A scoped probe re-ran the previously-exhausted GPQA items under both greedy and MiniMax's sampling at a fixed 32k budget. The result splits cleanly by arm — non-termination rate on that stuck-item subset:

cyankiwi AWQ94.0%87.3%−7 pts

Sampling does not reduce exhaustion on BF16 or GPTQ — confirming their overruns are genuinely hard items, not loops (a clean control that the test isn't just a termination knob). It substantially rescues our AWQ: non-termination drops 76% → 49%, and 74% of the stuck items terminate on at least one sampled attempt (mean 51% of attempts). So a large share of our AWQ regression is a greedy × quant artifact — the true deployment-mode (sampling) damage is smaller than the greedy scores imply, though not zero. The community cyankiwi quant barely moves (94% → 87%; only 30% of items ever terminate): its degenerate single-token loops are robust to sampling and represent genuine, severe damage.

From empty answer to correct answer

Terminating is necessary but not sufficient — do the recovered answers get the question right? On the exhausted subset (the hardest GPQA items, empty under the original greedy run), sampling converts a large share of dead responses into correct ones. "Per draw" is the honest single-sample expectation at temperature 1.0; "≥1 of 3" is the optimistic pass@3.

For our AWQ, per-draw accuracy on these previously-dead items rises from 24% (greedy) to 42% under sampling, and 60% are answered correctly on at least one of three draws. Crucially, when a sampled attempt does terminate it is correct 83% of the time — matching the healthy arms (BF16 86%, GPTQ 90%). So the bottleneck is purely getting the model to stop: AWQ loses termination, not correctness. cyankiwi stays dead (5% per draw, only 12% ever correct) because it almost never terminates.

Scope & caveats

These figures cover the exhausted subset only (the hardest items) — they are not the overall task-score change under sampling, which requires a full re-run and remains open. "Correct" is best-effort GPQA answer-letter extraction (approximate). The greedy column is a fresh 32k re-run, so it terminates a little (run-to-run nondeterminism); these same items scored zero in the actual eval.

What this meant for the verdict — and what happened next

At the time, the r5 regression split into a greedy-induced component (apparently fixable decode-side) and a residual recipe component, with GPQA and IFEval sharing one root cause so a single fix would address both. That is what the fix delivered: the recipe component turned out to be the up→down fold, and removing it collapsed both tasks' token spend and exhaustion back to baseline — see the r6/r7 section. The decode-side workaround was never needed in production. Thinking stays on throughout: it is the standard, correct setting for a reasoning model, and disabling it would both break convention and hide a real deployment risk. Full reasoning transcripts for every exhausted request are captured, so the loops can be inspected without a rerun.
